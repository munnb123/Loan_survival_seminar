\begin{frame}{A binary label needs a horizon}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{.6cm}
\item<1-> A classifier can predict default within 12 months.
\item<2-> Six months without default does not establish a 12-month label.
\end{itemize}
\vspace{.5cm}
\qual{Training and evaluation labels need appropriate follow-up.}
\talknote{1:00}{A conventional classifier can answer a perfectly legitimate fixed-horizon question, such as default within twelve months. The issue is not that classification is wrong: it is that a label requires the relevant horizon to be observed, or otherwise appropriately handled. An event observed before the horizon is known; a no-default label generally needs exposure through that horizon, with competing events addressed according to the intended target. Our six-month borrower does not yet supply that label. If already running long, skip the next worked-label slide and go directly to slide 6; this explanation is self-contained.}

\begin{frame}[label=why-survival]{Why survival analysis?}
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{.55cm}
\item<1-> Use when events occur.
\item<2-> Accommodate right-censored follow-up under suitable assumptions.
\item<3-> Here: Cox for interpretable relative default hazards.
\end{itemize}
\vspace{.35cm}\qual{Machine-learning methods can also model survival outcomes.}
\talknote{0:55}{Connect both opening problems to the method. Time-to-event data retain event timing and can use incomplete right-censored histories under appropriate observation assumptions. This does not mean censoring can be ignored or that survival analysis is automatically unbiased. We choose Cox because a small set of relative-hazard effects is interpretable for this lecture. Survival analysis is a type of outcome framework, not the opposite of machine learning; ML survival methods also exist. We will not compare their predictive performance.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The survival function}
\qual{First use a hypothetical default-only setting.}
\vspace{.6cm}
{\Large\[S(t)=P(T_D>t)\]}
\vspace{.6cm}
\uncover<2->{The probability of remaining default-free beyond time $t$.}
\talknote{0:45}{We now have a reason to introduce notation. Let T_D be time to default in a simplified world without competing closure. Read S(t) as the probability that the event has not happened by t. Reveal the plain-language interpretation after reading the equation. Survival is the technical term for remaining event-free; it does not imply anything about the borrower outside this defined event.}

\begin{frame}{From a short interval to a hazard rate}
\qual{Hypothetical default-only setting; $t$ in months.}
\vspace{.4cm}
\[
h(t)=\lim_{\Delta t\downarrow 0}
\frac{P(t\leq T_D<t+\Delta t\mid T_D\geq t)}{\Delta t}
\]
\vspace{.4cm}
\uncover<2->{\[
P(t\leq T_D<t+\Delta t\mid T_D\geq t)
\ \approx\ h(t)\,\Delta t
\]}
\uncover<2->{\textcolor{teal}{Rate $\times$ a short interval $\approx$ a conditional probability.}}
\talknote{1:20}{Read the numerator first: conditional on no default yet, what is the chance of default in the next small interval? Divide by its length and shrink the interval to obtain a rate. Do not explain every symbol as a separate reveal. Once the definition is explained, reveal the short-interval approximation. For the earlier curve a constant hazard of 0.02 per month gives an approximate 0.02 one-month conditional probability, with exact value 1-exp(-0.02), about 0.0198. Do not multiply a rate over a long horizon as though it were an exact probability. If running long, skip slide 13 and go directly to borrower characteristics on slide 14.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}[label=optional-cumulative]{From hazard to survival}
\optionalexplanation\quad\qual{Default-only setting.}
\vspace{.25cm}
{\Large\[H(t)=\int_0^t h(u)\,du\]}
\uncover<2->{{\Large\[S(t)=\exp\{-H(t)\}\]}}
\vspace{.35cm}
\uncover<2->{\qual{With competing closure, default hazard alone does not determine actual default probabilities.}}
\talknote{0:45}{OPTIONAL: skip this entire slide to save 45 seconds. It connects two already introduced objects. H accumulates the hazard over time and, in the default-only setting, S equals exp(-H). For the illustrative constant rate, H(t)=0.02t, giving the earlier curve. Reveal the survival equation only after the integral. With competing closure, exp(-H_D) is not the actual event-free survival or actual default probability: those depend on the competing process too. No cumulative-incidence analysis is being added. Continue with slide 14; it does not rely on H notation.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Cox model}
\vspace{.1cm}
{\Large\[
h(t\mid x)=\underbrace{h_0(t)}_{\text{baseline}}
\underbrace{\exp(\beta^{\mathsf T}x)}_{\text{multiplier}}
\]}
\vspace{.3cm}
\uncover<2->{Baseline: the time pattern for reference covariates.}\par
\vspace{.3cm}
\uncover<3->{Multiplier: how the covariates scale that rate.}\par
\vspace{.4cm}
\qual{For credit: a cause-specific default rate among loans still exposed.}
\talknote{1:20}{Show the whole equation first and read it as baseline times a positive covariate multiplier. Reveal the baseline explanation and point to h_0(t): we do not require a constant hazard or choose a fixed parametric shape here. It represents the rate at reference covariates. Then reveal the multiplier explanation: exp(beta transpose x) scales that rate. In the audited example the object is the cause-specific default hazard among loans neither defaulted nor closed, and the baseline is allowed to differ by origination year. Borrower coefficients are shared across those strata.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Compare otherwise identical borrowers}
\qual{Hypothetical new vs existing customer; same baseline and other covariates.}
\vspace{.45cm}
\[
\mathrm{HR}=\frac{h(t\mid\text{new})}{h(t\mid\text{existing})}
=\exp(\beta_{\mathrm{new}})
\]
\vspace{.5cm}
\uncover<2->{If HR $=1.5$: a 50\% higher rate at each $t$, under PH.}\par
\vspace{.4cm}
\qual{A conditional rate ratio, not a probability ratio or causal effect.}
\talknote{1:00}{The baseline cancels for two otherwise identical borrowers in the same baseline stratum. The customer indicator differs by one, so their relative hazard is exp(beta_new). Explain the equation before revealing the illustrative value of 1.5. That means a fifty percent higher instantaneous rate, not fifty percentage points or a fifty percent higher default probability over a horizon. This value is hypothetical and is not the final Bondora estimate. Under PH the ratio remains constant, even though both hazards may vary, as the next picture shows.}
\end{frame}
